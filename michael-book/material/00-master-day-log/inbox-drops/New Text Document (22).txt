Paradoxical thinkingParadoxical thinking is the capacity (and deliberate practice) of engaging productively with contradictions, tensions, and apparent opposites rather than immediately resolving them into a binary choice or dismissing one side.
It is not the same as logical contradiction (which violates the law of non-contradiction). Instead, it treats certain opposites as coexisting at different levels, in different contexts, or as generative tensions that can produce insight, creativity, or change.
The term appears in several related but distinct domains:
1. The Paradox Mindset / Janusian Thinking (Creativity & Performance)
This is the most broadly useful modern sense. Psychologist Albert Rothenberg called it janusian thinking (after the two-faced Roman god Janus): the active conception of two or more opposite or antithetical ideas, concepts, or images simultaneously.
Research by Ella Miron-Spektor and colleagues shows that people who can hold opposing demands (explore and exploit, compete and collaborate, stability and change) tend to be more creative, flexible, and productive—especially under tension or scarcity. Simply priming people by asking them to list paradoxical statements (e.g., “sitting can be more tiring than walking”) improved performance on insight problems such as the classic candle problem (35% success vs. 21% in controls).
Classic examples include:

Einstein treating an object as simultaneously at rest and in motion depending on the frame of reference.
Niels Bohr’s complementarity principle (light as both wave and particle).
Organizational polarities that high-performing companies deliberately manage rather than eliminate (Toyota’s simultaneous emphasis on stability and continuous reform).

F. Scott Fitzgerald captured the spirit: “The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in the mind at the same time, and still retain the ability to function.”
2. Paradoxical Intention (Therapy)
Viktor Frankl’s logotherapeutic technique. When someone suffers from anticipatory anxiety (fear of stuttering, sweating, insomnia, trembling, etc.), the therapist instructs them to intentionally try to produce or exaggerate the feared symptom—often with humor.
The goal is to break the vicious cycle of “trying hard not to X makes X more likely.” Classic cases:

A man terrified of excessive sweating is told to try to sweat “at least ten quarts.”
Someone who stutters is instructed to stutter as much as possible on purpose.
An insomniac is told to try as hard as possible to stay awake.

By replacing fear with a paradoxical wish, the symptom often loses its power.
3. Paradoxical Thinking as Attitude-Change Intervention
Developed especially in intergroup conflict research (Hameiri, Bar-Tal, Halperin and colleagues), this approach presents people with messages that agree with their existing beliefs but push them to an extreme or absurd degree. The aim is “cognitive unfreezing”: the person distances themselves from the exaggerated version and thereby softens their original stance.
It draws on the classical rhetorical technique of reductio ad absurdum. Recent experimental work (including a 2026 study) has applied it to morally charged social attitudes with measurable effects. It is more effective with people who hold strong, identity-linked views that resist direct counter-argument, but it requires careful calibration and raises ethical questions about manipulation.
4. Personal & Organizational Peak Performance
Jerry Fletcher and Kelle Olwyler’s work on Paradoxical Thinking argues that individuals perform at their best when they consciously harness their own core contradictions (e.g., being simultaneously aggressive and relaxed, visionary and practical). Leadership literature on “polarity management” makes the same point at the organizational level: many important tensions are not problems to solve but polarities to manage continuously.
How to Cultivate It

Notice either/or assumptions in your own thinking and deliberately reframe them as both/and.
Practice generating paradoxical statements or listing the opposing demands in a situation you face.
In decision-making or creative work, temporarily hold the tension instead of collapsing it too quickly.
Observe real-world systems that successfully manage polarities (nature, successful companies, scientific theories).

Paradoxical thinking is not a rejection of logic or clarity. It is an expansion of it—an acknowledgment that some of the most important truths, creative breakthroughs, and psychological shifts live in the space where apparent opposites can both be true.
