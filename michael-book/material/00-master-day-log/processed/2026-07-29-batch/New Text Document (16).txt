The distinction between either/or thinking and both/and thinking shows up across philosophy, psychology, systems theory, leadership literature, and organizational development.
Either/Or thinking
This is binary, dichotomous reasoning. It treats options as mutually exclusive: A or B, true or false, good or bad, us or them. It is rooted in classical Western logic (Aristotle’s law of non-contradiction and the excluded middle).
It is powerful when you need clarity, speed, or a decisive choice under simple or high-stakes conditions. The downside is that it easily creates false dilemmas, polarizes discussion, and flattens complexity.
Both/And thinking
This mode holds apparent opposites at the same time. It treats many tensions as interdependent polarities rather than problems to be solved by picking a side. Classic expressions appear in:

Dialectical traditions (Hegel and later developments)
Eastern non-dual frameworks (yin-yang, middle way)
Modern systems and complexity thinking
Psychological approaches such as Dialectical Behavior Therapy (which explicitly trains people to hold acceptance and change)

In management and leadership writing the same idea appears as:

Jim Collins & Jerry Porras’s “Genius of the AND” versus the “Tyranny of the OR” (Built to Last)
Roger Martin’s integrative thinking (The Opposable Mind)
Barry Johnson’s polarity management
Wendy Smith & Marianne Lewis’s recent book Both/And Thinking (which frames many organizational dilemmas as paradoxes best navigated rather than resolved by choosing one pole)

Everyday examples of both/and polarities include stability and change, focus and openness, short-term results and long-term health, self and other, structure and flexibility.
Practical takeaway
Neither mode is universally superior. Either/or is adaptive for discrete decisions and clear threats. In a standard modern translation (NIV):
“Enter through the narrow gate. For wide is the gate and broad is the road that leads to destruction, and many enter through it. But small is the gate and narrow the road that leads to life, and only a few find it.”
Other renderings use “strait is the gate,” “the way is hard,” or “constricted,” but the core image is the same: two gates, two roads, two destinations. One is wide, easy, and crowded; the other is narrow, difficult, and sparsely traveled.
In the Christian tradition this is usually read as an exclusive claim about the path to “life” (often understood as eternal life or entrance into the kingdom). Jesus presents himself elsewhere as the only way (John 14:6), and the narrowness is taken to reflect both the exclusivity of that claim and the costly nature of genuine discipleship — repentance, obedience, and resistance to the easier, more popular alternatives. The “few” underscores that it is not the majority path.
The verse is a classic example of either/or framing: two mutually exclusive options with no middle ground offered. That sits in interesting tension with the both/and mode of thinking we were discussing earlier, which tends to hold polarities rather than force a binary choice.
