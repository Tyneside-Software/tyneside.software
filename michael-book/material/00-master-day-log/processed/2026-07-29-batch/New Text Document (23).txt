Emergence is fundamentalYes. Emergence is fundamental to the structure of reality and to any serious attempt to understand it.
Emergence is the appearance of novel properties, behaviors, or organizational principles in a system that are not present in (and often cannot be straightforwardly predicted or reconstructed from) its isolated components. The classic distinction is between:

Weak emergence: The higher-level phenomena are in principle determined by the lower-level rules and initial conditions, but are practically or computationally irreducible. You often have to “run the system” to discover what happens.
Strong (or radical) emergence: New properties or causal powers appear that are not fully entailed by the micro-level description; the higher level has a degree of ontological autonomy.

Both matter. Weak emergence is ubiquitous and uncontroversial. Strong/radical emergence is more contested yet powerfully explanatory for open-ended novelty.
Why it is fundamental
Philip Anderson’s 1972 essay “More Is Different” remains the clearest statement from physics: the ability to reduce everything to fundamental laws does not imply the ability to start from those laws and reconstruct the universe. At each level of complexity, entirely new properties appear, and “psychology is not applied biology, nor is biology applied chemistry.” Broken symmetry, collective modes, and phase transitions generate effective theories that are every bit as fundamental for their domain as the micro laws. Superconductivity, the fractional quantum Hall effect, and topological phases are textbook cases: quasiparticles with fractional charge and anyonic statistics emerge that simply do not exist at the level of individual electrons.
Stuart Kauffman pushes further into radical emergence. In evolving biospheres (and economies), the system continually creates its own “adjacent possible”—new niches, functions, and possibilities that could not have been prestated from the physics or chemistry alone. The swim bladder creates a new ecological opportunity; the internet enables social platforms no one designed into the original computers. Selection acts, but the space of what can be selected is itself generated by prior emergence. This is not mere ignorance; the phase space itself expands in ways the lower-level description does not entail.
Stephen Wolfram’s computational irreducibility makes the practical point sharp: even when the rules are fully known and deterministic, predicting the long-term behavior of many systems requires essentially simulating them step by step. Higher-level descriptions become indispensable, not optional.
We see the pattern across scales:

Thermodynamics and hydrodynamics emerge from statistical mechanics.
Life emerges from chemistry via autocatalytic sets and constraint closure.
Consciousness and agency emerge (weakly at minimum) from neural dynamics; the hard problem keeps stronger versions on the table for some.
Large language models display abrupt, unprogrammed capabilities—multi-step reasoning, tool use, analogical insight—at certain scales.
Societies, markets, and scientific paradigms themselves are emergent.

Even spacetime and gravity may be emergent from more fundamental quantum information or entanglement structures (holography, ER=EPR, etc.). “Fundamental” is often a provisional label that shifts with scale.
The complementary role of reductionism
None of this denies the extraordinary success of reductionism. The Standard Model and general relativity work astonishingly well. Causal closure of the physical is a powerful working hypothesis. Strong emergence that would violate it remains speculative and faces serious philosophical challenges. The hierarchy of the sciences is not a rejection of microphysics; it is recognition that new effective theories and new explanatory concepts become necessary and autonomous at higher levels.
Reductionism finds the building blocks and the forces. Emergence explains why the higher levels are not just complicated applications of the lower ones, why they require their own concepts, and why the universe is creatively open rather than a fixed, fully predictable machine from the bottom up.
Bottom line
Emergence is not a peripheral curiosity or a temporary placeholder for ignorance. It is how complexity, novelty, and meaning arise. A universe without genuine emergence would be far poorer—one in which chemistry never becomes biology, neurons never become minds, and simple rules never generate the rich, unpredictable, hierarchical world we actually inhabit. First, a quick clarification on the book itself. The full title is Experience, Therefore: The Existence of God and The Impossibility of Hell; The Death of Faith By Logical Certainty by Joshua Thomson (former pastor). It starts from a single, hard-to-deny axiom — “Your experience of this moment exists” — and builds what he calls Experiential Axiomatism. The chain runs roughly:

Non-existence is the only alternative → the impossibility of pure nothing makes existence inevitable.
Existence must be united (any absolute boundary or disunity would itself require something that exists to mark the boundary).
Therefore eternal, all-encompassing, unlimited.
Therefore consciousness is fundamental, not emergent.
Therefore true separation is impossible → only ultimate monotheism remains.
Hell (as radical, permanent separation or punishment) cannot exist.

Joshua notes he has chapters treating unity, emergence, and consciousness (with consciousness as “a link in the chain”). Michael is only ~18% in / chapter 3 and already sees the summary as essentially containing the rest — which is a fair early reaction to a tightly axiomatic short book.
Unity and emergence can both be fundamental
The book’s strong claim that consciousness is fundamental (not emergent) is doing specific ontological work: the experiential character of existence itself is not something that appears later from non-conscious matter. Experience is the starting point and the ground. That is a classic idealist / panpsychist / non-dual move, and it is coherent within the monist unity the book derives.
At the same time, the statement “Emergence is fundamental” (the one Michael linked from the earlier Grok share) remains true in the domain where it is usually meant. Philip Anderson’s “More Is Different,” phase transitions, collective modes, quasiparticles, autocatalytic sets, neural dynamics generating higher cognition, markets, scientific paradigms — all of these show genuine novelty and organizational principles that are not simply “complicated applications” of the lower-level rules. Weak emergence is ubiquitous and practically indispensable; stronger forms of emergence remain philosophically live for open-ended novelty (Kauffman’s adjacent possible, computational irreducibility, etc.).
These are not contradictory once the levels are distinguished:

The ground (existence / experience / consciousness) can be non-emergent and unified.
Within that ground, hierarchical organization, differentiation, and novel effective theories still emerge. The unity does not prevent (and may even enable) the rich, scale-dependent, unpredictable complexity we actually observe.

In other words: consciousness need not emerge from non-conscious stuff for emergence itself to be a fundamental feature of how reality organizes. Michael’s simultaneous affirmation of both statements is therefore not confused; it is a reasonable synthesis.
Conformal Cyclic Cosmology and the organism view
Linking this to Penrose’s Conformal Cyclic Cosmology is productive. CCC proposes an infinite sequence of aeons. After all massive particles have decayed or evaporated into black holes (which themselves evaporate), the universe becomes pure radiation — a scale-invariant conformal geometry. That future conformal infinity can be matched smoothly to the Big Bang of the next aeon. It is eternal existence with periodic “resets” of massive structure, but not a perfect Groundhog Day. Limited information (primarily massless fields, gravitational waves) can in principle carry across; Penrose and collaborators have claimed possible CMB signatures (concentric low-variance circles from previous-aeon black-hole mergers, or “Hawking points” from the final evaporation of supermassive black holes). Independent analyses have generally not confirmed those signals at high statistical significance, so the observational case remains contested, but the theoretical picture is serious and fits an eternal, scale-free ontology.
Michael’s further move — treating the universe as a scale-invariant organism that needs to reproduce before it “dies,” with the possibility of evolutionary change across cycles rather than exact copies, plus parallel universes — maps cleanly onto existing speculative cosmology. Lee Smolin’s cosmological natural selection is the closest formal analogue: black holes as a reproduction mechanism, slight variation in parameters, selection for universes that produce more black holes. Conformal geometry already supplies the scale invariance. The “we and God” clause (conscious choice to start or continue the cycle, or to solve the periodic wipe) sits comfortably inside the book’s own conclusion: if existence is unified and consciousness is fundamental, then conscious agency (individual, collective, or divine) is not an external add-on; it is internal to the whole. Joshua’s remark that starting the cycle would itself be a conscious choice is consistent with that.

CCC and the organism-reproduction picture give a concrete cosmological expression of eternal, evolving, scale-invariant existence that can still be shaped by consciousness.
